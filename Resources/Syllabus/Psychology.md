# Psychology (SL/HL)
Group 3 (Individuals and societies) · new guide, first teaching 2025, first assessment 2027 · SL 150 h, HL 240 h
Aliases: Psychology, Psych, Psychology HL, Psychology SL
Assessment details below are a summary in our words and unverified: check paper formats and marks against the school's guide or the teacher before relying on them.

## How it's structured
The course links four things, and questions are asked through them:
- Concepts (6): bias, causality, change, measurement, perspective, responsibility.
- Content: the biological, cognitive and sociocultural approaches to behaviour, plus research methods and ethics.
- Contexts (4): health and well-being, human development, human relationships, learning and cognition.
- HL extensions: the role of culture, of motivation and of technology in behaviour (HL only).
Assessment (outline): exam papers with concept-based short answers and essays on a context, a paper on research methods and data from an unseen study, an extra HL paper on the extensions, and an internal assessment built around a research study the student designs and runs. Essays are marked on knowledge and understanding, use of research, critical thinking and organization.

## Approaches and the research to know
### Biological approach
- Brain and behaviour: localization (Broca, Wernicke, HM: Milner and Scoville), neuroplasticity (Maguire 2000 taxi drivers, Draganski 2004 juggling), techniques (fMRI, PET, EEG, lesion studies)
- Neurotransmitters and behaviour: serotonin, dopamine, acetylcholine (Antonova 2011 scopolamine), agonists and antagonists
- Hormones and pheromones: cortisol and stress, oxytocin and trust (Kosfeld 2005), testosterone, pheromones and attraction (Wedekind 1995 sweaty T-shirt, its limits)
- Genetics and behaviour: twin, family and adoption studies, heritability, the 5-HTT gene and depression (Caspi 2003), evolutionary explanations, animal research and its ethics

### Cognitive approach
- Models of memory: multi-store model (Atkinson and Shiffrin; Glanzer and Cunitz 1966), working memory model (Baddeley and Hitch; Landry and Bartling 2011)
- Schema theory: Bartlett 1932 War of the Ghosts, Anderson and Pichert 1978, Brewer and Treyens 1981
- Thinking and decision-making: dual-process model (system 1 and 2), heuristics and biases (Tversky and Kahneman, anchoring, framing), rational vs intuitive thinking
- Reliability of cognitive processes: reconstructive memory and eyewitness testimony (Loftus and Palmer 1974), confirmation bias
- Emotion and cognition: flashbulb memory (Brown and Kulik 1977, Neisser and Harsch 1992, Talarico and Rubin 2003)
- Cognition in the digital world: effects of technology on attention and memory (Sparrow 2011 "Google effect")

### Sociocultural approach
- The individual and the group: social identity theory (Tajfel 1971 minimal groups), social cognitive theory (Bandura 1961 Bobo doll), stereotypes and their formation (illusory correlation: Hamilton and Gifford 1976; stereotype threat: Steele and Aronson 1995)
- Cultural origins of behaviour and cognition: culture and cultural norms, cultural dimensions (Hofstede; individualism vs collectivism), enculturation, acculturation (Berry's model)
- Conformity and compliance (Asch 1951, Bond and Smith 1996), obedience (Milgram 1963)

### Research methods and ethics
- Quantitative: true, quasi- and natural experiments, field experiments, correlational studies; IV, DV, controls, operationalization, hypotheses (null and alternative), sampling (random, opportunity, self-selected, purposive, snowball), design (independent samples, repeated measures, matched pairs), internal and external validity, demand characteristics, counterbalancing
- Qualitative: interviews (structured, semi-structured, focus groups), observations (naturalistic, covert, participant), case studies; credibility, transferability, researcher bias, triangulation, reflexivity
- Data: descriptive statistics (mean, median, standard deviation), graphs, inferential tests and significance (p < 0.05), correlation vs causation, effect size
- Ethics: informed consent, deception, debriefing, right to withdraw, confidentiality, protection from harm; ethics in animal research

## Contexts with common topics
- Health and well-being: stress (biological, cognitive and social factors), explanations and treatments of depression (biological: SSRIs; cognitive: Beck, CBT; sociocultural: Brown and Harris 1978), prevalence and diagnosis (validity, reliability, cultural bias), health promotion
- Human development: brain development, cognitive development (Piaget; Vygotsky), attachment (Bowlby, Ainsworth's Strange Situation, cross-cultural variation: van IJzendoorn), identity development, effects of poverty and trauma
- Human relationships: formation of relationships (attraction, similarity, proximity), communication, conflict and its resolution (Sherif 1954 Robbers Cave, contact hypothesis), prosocial behaviour (bystander effect: Darley and Latané 1968; empathy–altruism)
- Learning and cognition: classical and operant conditioning, social learning, memory and learning strategies, cognitive biases in learning

## How to quiz it
- Short answers: "Describe one study related to…", "Explain one concept…" (about 4–6 marks): aim, method, findings, conclusion, then the explicit link to the question.
- Essays: "Evaluate…", "Discuss…", "To what extent…" (about 15–22 marks): a clear argument, two or more studies used as evidence (not just described), evaluation of methods (strengths, limitations, ethics, culture, generalizability), alternative explanations, a reasoned conclusion. Link to one of the six concepts when the question names one.
- Research methods drills: give a short invented study (e.g. 40 students, music vs silence while learning word lists) and ask for the design, a hypothesis, a weakness in sampling, an ethical issue, or what a p-value of 0.03 means.
- Mark studies on accuracy (researcher, year, method, sample, findings) and on how well they're applied to the question; describing a study without linking it earns little.
- Common slips to probe: claiming correlation proves cause, mixing up reliability and validity, forgetting the ethics of deception in Milgram, overgeneralizing from WEIRD samples, "proves" instead of "supports".
